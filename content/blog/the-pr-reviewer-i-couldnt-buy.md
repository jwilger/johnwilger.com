I asked Claude a feasibility question I fully expected it to answer "no."

I'd only started using [CodeRabbit](https://www.coderabbit.ai/) on my personal GitHub repos a couple of weeks before this, on the recommendation of colleagues who'd been quietly evangelizing it for months. Within the first few PRs, I understood what they'd been on about. It was good. Visibly better, and substantially cheaper, than the homegrown Claude/OpenAI-reviewer-in-CI rig I'd been operating before that, where the cloud-API bill was creeping into territory no individual ought to be paying out of pocket for code review on a hobby repo. CodeRabbit's pricing is, to its credit, fair. I was getting more useful review per dollar than I had from the DIY setup, with none of the maintenance overhead. I'd just gotten to the point of quietly trusting it.

Then I moved my repos off GitHub.

CodeRabbit doesn't run on Forgejo. Nothing comparable does. The major commercial AI reviewers — CodeRabbit, [Greptile](https://www.greptile.com/), Graphite's [Graphite Chat](https://diamond.graphite.dev/) (formerly Diamond), [Cursor's Bugbot](https://cursor.com/bugbot), and Copilot's review feature — collectively support every other significant forge: GitHub, plus various combinations of GitHub Enterprise Server, GitLab.com, self-managed GitLab, Azure DevOps, and Bitbucket, depending on the product. CodeRabbit's documentation lists seven supported platforms, which appears to be the most coverage of any of them.[^coderabbit-platforms] None of them, CodeRabbit included, runs on Gitea or Forgejo, and as of this writing none has publicly signaled an intent to. The most prominent open-source alternative, [Qodo's PR-Agent](https://github.com/qodo-ai/pr-agent), does have a Gitea provider in its supported-platforms list, and so may, in principle, work against Forgejo (which is a hard fork of Gitea), though I haven't tested it. The trade-off there is architectural rather than commercial. PR-Agent's project README describes its design as "each tool (`/review`, `/improve`, `/ask`) uses a single LLM call (~30 seconds, low cost)," with no bundled linter pipeline, no sandboxed execution of those linters or of LLM-issued tool calls, no persistent learnings store, and no separate verification pass. That's a perfectly reasonable shape for a project that values simplicity over feature parity. It isn't the shape of the systems I missed.

So I typed something like "what would it take to reimplement CodeRabbit for Forgejo" into Claude Code and went to make coffee, expecting to come back to a polite list of reasons why this was a multi-quarter, team-of-engineers project I had no business attempting alone.

That's not what I came back to. Twelve hours later I had a working PR reviewer running against this very repository, posting inline comments on its own commits.

This article has four threads running through it. The first is why I left GitHub, framed by the specific bad week during which I left. The second is how a language model reverse-engineered a closed-source product from its creators' own public marketing. The third is what came out of that reverse-engineering: [`auto_review`](https://git.johnwilger.com/jwilger/auto_review), the system I'm releasing. The fourth is the part I didn't see coming when I started, and which this essay grew to make room for: the techniques I had to compose to make any of this work, which generalize well beyond code review and which I think are more useful than the code-review tool itself. If you're considering an exit from GitHub, the next section is for you. If you're working on agentic systems and don't care which forge anyone uses, jump to "What Generalizes." If you're CodeRabbit-curious, the middle is yours.

## Why I Left

GitHub used to feel like a place that liked developers. Some of you remember. Octocat stickers, social coding rhetoric, the sense that the people running the company shared the values of the people using it. A tool built by people who shipped software, for people who shipped software.

That hasn't been true for some time, and the trajectory keeps bending the same way. Every quarter brings another line item that reads less like "feature" and more like "monetization surface." Every UI redesign loses functionality I used. Every "AI-powered" announcement shifts more of my code, my issues, my private workflow into the training pipeline of a vendor whose interests no longer obviously align with mine. Cory Doctorow has a word for this — enshittification[^doctorow-2023] — and once you know the shape of it, you start seeing it everywhere. The platform optimizes for users, then for business customers, then for shareholders, and the users who built the network in the first place become the substrate that gets ground up to feed the next quarter's growth metric.

You can argue with the framing in the abstract. The numbers are harder to argue with. The decline shows up early in the public record. A June 2020 analysis by StatusGator,[^statusgator-2020] comparing the twenty-four months before Microsoft's June 2018 acquisition announcement to the twenty-four months after, found a 41% increase in published status-page incidents and a 97% increase in incident-minutes — 12,074 minutes of downtime in the post-acquisition window versus 6,110 in the pre-acquisition window. The trend has not reversed since. According to a reconstructed monthly-uptime tracker maintained by Marek Šuppa[^suppa-tracker] and cited by *The Register*,[^register-2026] GitHub's monthly uptime dropped below 90% in 2025 and has continued to slide. As of late April 2026, monthly uptime is reported as below 85%. On April 28, GitHub's CTO Vlad Fedorov posted a public statement[^github-availability-2026] acknowledging that recent incidents "are not acceptable" and apologizing for the impact, while attributing the underlying load increase to "agentic development workflows" that have "accelerated sharply" since late 2025. That explanation isn't wrong. It also isn't a defense. "Our customers are using our product more" is a description of GitHub's job, not an excuse for failing at it. And this is Microsoft, not a seed-funded startup encountering scale for the first time. They've been running consumer and enterprise services at hyperscale for decades. They own a cloud business measured in the tens of billions of dollars. The institutional memory, hiring budget, and operational playbooks needed to anticipate a load curve like this are supposed to be there. "We didn't see it coming" is not an answer this owner has earned the right to give.

The reliability story is, in some ways, the least of it. Consider the week immediately before I started writing this essay:

- **April 23rd**: a merge-queue regression silently reverted code in 2,092 pull requests across 658 repositories.[^github-incident-193645][^github-availability-2026] Squash-merge groups containing more than one PR produced incorrect three-way merges; previously merged commits were retroactively un-merged into the resulting commit. The bug landed because a feature flag's gating was incomplete. It went undetected by GitHub's own monitoring because the symptom wasn't unavailability but data-integrity corruption, and was only found when customers started filing tickets. As Gergely Orosz put it on X,[^orosz-tweet] this was "not an outage resulting in downtime, or data lost on the cloud (that is trivial to restore from local git). It's a data integrity issue, which sounds hard and difficult to untangle by anyone hit by it." The mental contract you have with your version-control host, that a merge means what the merge says, broke for those teams, retroactively, without warning.
- **April 27th through May 1st**: GitHub's Elasticsearch subsystem fell over. Per GitHub's own April 28 statement,[^github-availability-2026] the cluster "became overloaded (likely due to a botnet attack) and stopped returning search results." The user-visible recovery,[^isdown-577936] spanning roughly 62 hours from late on April 28 until 04:15 UTC on May 1, required GitHub to reindex pull-request data and repair missing and stale search records. For nearly three days, any UI surface that depended on PR search returned incomplete or empty results. The underlying data wasn't lost; it just wasn't findable, which on a forge is functionally the same thing for triage, review, and CI. If you wanted to know whether there was an open PR touching a file, you couldn't.
- **April 29th**: the TanStack supply-chain incident became public.[^socket-tanstack] According to Socket's published research, the holder of the unscoped `tanstack` name on npm (a registry GitHub has owned since its 2020 acquisition) used it to publish four malicious versions (2.0.4 through 2.0.7) in a 27-minute window, each running a postinstall script that collected `.env`, `.env.local`, `.env.production`, and similar files from the installing machine and POSTed them to a Svix-hosted endpoint operated by the attacker. In a public statement Socket attributes to him,[^socket-tanstack-tweet] Tanner Linsley, the maintainer of the legitimate `@tanstack/*` packages, said TanStack had filed legal documents related to a pending trademark-infringement claim against the squatter, that the squatter had previously demanded $10,000 from him, and that TanStack had "repeatedly tried, unsuccessfully, to get @npmjs to address the situation." The package shipped, developers installed it, and secrets walked out the front door.

You can read those three things as bad luck in a single bad week. I read them as a pattern. A registry that won't enforce its own brand-impersonation rules until something explodes. A forge whose merge primitives can corrupt without alerting. A search system that can fall over for half a week. Each one is a single failure. Together they describe an institution whose ratio of things shipping to things being maintained has visibly slipped, on the watch of an owner whose other recent flagships, Windows and Xbox, are getting the same kind of public eulogy from longtime users.[^windowscentral-2026] The simplest explanation is the older one: large companies acquire beloved products, optimize them for the acquirer's quarterly story rather than the user's daily one, and the slope only goes one way.

I am, for what it's worth, GitHub user ID 1654. I signed up on February 28, 2008, while the site was still in beta and roughly six weeks before its public launch in April 2008.[^wikipedia-github] That's not a credential, it's a tenure. And I'll say what I think a lot of people in the four-digit-user-ID club are feeling and not quite saying out loud: it makes me sad. GitHub at its founding was one of those rare products made by developers for developers, with the kind of taste and care that only happens when the people building the thing are also the people using it daily. I met collaborators there. I learned in public there. I shipped code I'm proud of there. Watching that institution turn into a slowly-degrading dashboard for an absentee owner's quarterly narrative is a particular kind of grief, the same one you feel when a neighborhood place gets bought by a chain, or a band you loved has one too many lineup changes. The thing isn't gone, exactly. It's just no longer the thing.

I held out a long time after I'd stopped enjoying it. What finally pushed me to do something about it was, ironically, an AI feature. I noticed I was reflexively double-checking that I'd opted out of one training-data setting after another on a service that kept silently re-enrolling me, and I asked myself why I was paying someone money to do this to me.

So I stood up [Forgejo](https://forgejo.org) on a NixOS box of mine. Forgejo is a community-led fork of Gitea, itself a Go-based GitHub-style forge that's been quietly excellent for years. Forgejo was created in late 2022 after Gitea's then-lead maintainer transferred the project's trademarks and operations into a for-profit corporation and began moving toward an open-core model.[^wikipedia-forgejo] The non-profit [Codeberg e.V.](https://codeberg.org/) is now the de-jure lead maintainer, and the politics of the project line up with mine: federation-friendly, sustainable governance, no VC pressure, and a structural answer to "what happens if the maintainer pivots to enterprise SaaS." The fork itself is the answer. The NixOS module is mercifully small. A `services.forgejo.enable = true` block, a Postgres database alongside it, an SSH-based commit-signing config so merge commits carry a real cryptographic identity rather than a bot's HTTP-Basic password, a couple of `lib.mkForce` overrides for the upstream module's overzealous `ProtectHome` hardening, and a Forgejo Actions runner for CI. That's most of it. My git lives at `git.johnwilger.com` now. SSH on 2222, HTTPS in front of the internal HTTP port, signing keys held by the `forgejo` system user. The whole thing is reproducible from a flake. If the box dies tomorrow, I rebuild it from declarative config, restore `/home/forgejo` from snapshots, and I'm back. There is no vendor in the loop.

The catch was that I'd just made my entire toolchain unrecognizable to every closed-source AI service I'd been using. The things that had been quietly making me better at my job didn't follow me out the door.

## The Question I Expected To Lose

For almost two years, I kept getting one thing wrong about LLMs. I treated them as encyclopedias. I'd ask them what they knew, get back the median of what their training data knew, and be disappointed when that turned out to be shallow.

What I hadn't internalized was that a current-generation reasoning model with web access and a serious context window isn't an encyclopedia. It's a research analyst that's read a lot and works fast. The interesting question isn't "what do you know about X" but "given everything publicly available about X, what is X actually doing, and could I build it?" Those are different questions, and they get different answers.

My initial prompt was lazy. I asked something to the effect of: is it feasible to build a CodeRabbit-equivalent for Forgejo, and if so, what would the architecture look like? I was expecting the model to push back on scope. Instead, it asked permission to dig, and then it dug.

What it surfaced over the next hour or so was something I should have realized earlier. CodeRabbit's founders and engineers had, by the time I asked, given a lot of public interviews about exactly how their system worked. There was a Software Engineering Daily interview with Harjot Gill[^se-daily-gill] walking through the multi-model pipeline architecture, the reasoning trail, and the context-window strategy. There was a Google Cloud case study[^google-cloud-coderabbit] detailing the use of Cloud Run for sandboxed execution: Cloud Tasks for queuing, [Jailkit](https://olivier.sessink.nl/jailkit/) for second-layer process isolation inside each microVM, dedicated service identities. There was a LanceDB case study[^lancedb-coderabbit] explaining how they used embedded vector storage to query "tens of thousands of tables" of PR, issue, code-dependency, and "tribal learnings" data with sub-second latency. There was an OpenAI customer story[^openai-coderabbit] (titled "Shipping code faster with o3, o4-mini, and GPT-4.1") naming the specific model mix CodeRabbit was running on, alongside CodeRabbit's own headline claims, surfaced in OpenAI's framing, of "4x faster" PR merges and "50% fewer bugs" in production. And there was Kudelski Security's August 2025 write-up[^kudelski-2025] of a vulnerability they had responsibly disclosed to CodeRabbit in January 2025, presented at Black Hat USA, in which the technical detail inadvertently confirmed dozens of CodeRabbit internals: the use of Rubocop in particular, the fact that at the time Rubocop was running outside the sandbox, and the `.rubocop.yml` `require: - ./ext.rb` extension directive the researchers used to load arbitrary Ruby and, by way of an environment variable containing CodeRabbit's GitHub-App private key, demonstrate read and write access to approximately one million repositories. CodeRabbit's public response[^coderabbit-kudelski-response] reports that Rubocop was disabled within an hour, credentials were rotated within three, the tool was relocated into the sandbox within twelve, and that the post-incident investigation found no evidence that any customer data was accessed.

In other words, you could triangulate the system. Not perfectly, not down to the last prompt, but down to the architectural decisions that mattered: the multi-stage pipeline rather than a pure ReAct loop, the cheap-tier triage model that drops "almost 50% on costs" per CodeRabbit's own engineering blog,[^coderabbit-cost-effective] the strict-JSON-schema review output with a self-heal validator, the cheap-tier verifier that drops unfounded findings, the per-repo "learnings" memory store, the 20+ bundled linters and security scanners that CodeRabbit's Cloud Run service runs on each PR,[^google-cloud-coderabbit] the inline-comment posting flow against the host's review API. The model assembled a nineteen-page feasibility study, complete with a phased build plan, cost estimates, a threat model, a list of files to create first, and an explicit list of what was out of scope. I read it skeptical, then less so once I'd checked the references it had pulled. They were real. The synthesis was sharp. The plan was something I could critique on its own terms — argue scope with, push back on, sharpen — rather than a mush of suggestions.

The phased plan estimated something like eighteen to twenty-four weeks of focused work to a CodeRabbit-equivalent, with a useful MVP at five weeks. That's roughly what a feasibility study by a careful senior engineer would produce. It was the right answer to the wrong question. The right question turned out to be: how fast can we put a thin end-to-end thread together and find out if any of this actually works?

## Twelve Hours

I started the workspace at 7:51 PM on April 30th. The first commit lays down the eleven-crate Rust workspace, the Dockerfile, the docker-compose template, the Forgejo Actions CI workflow, and the feasibility study itself, with a cargo-clean tree and a green `cargo check`.

By 7:55 PM, the Forgejo client was real: `get_pr_diff`, `list_changed_files`, `create_review`, `post_commit_status`, with seven `wiremock`-backed integration tests covering the API surface. By 8:00 PM, the LLM router was wired up against an OpenAI-compatible provider, with the tier-routing abstraction in place so a deployment can mix a local Ollama embedding model with a hosted reasoning model behind one config. By 8:12 PM, the review pipeline was emitting strict-JSON-schema-validated review comments through a self-heal loop, with fifteen failing tests landed before any of the implementation. By 8:18 PM, the orchestrator was wired through the gateway and a webhook actually triggered a review.

From `git init` to a webhook-triggered review pipeline in roughly twenty-seven minutes. The next several hours added the workspace clone, the linter fan-out, language-specific routing, the verification pass, and a lot more tests. By the next morning, `auto_review` was reviewing its own pull requests, and it has been ever since.

I'm posting the timestamps because I want to be precise about what changed.

What changed is not that I got faster at writing Rust. What changed is that the cost of getting from "is this even possible" to "I am looking at it work" collapsed to under a day. The feasibility study was the first artifact, not the last. The plan was a real plan — phased, defensible, and I'm still working through it — but I no longer had to wait until I'd finished it to find out whether the foundational claims were true. I could ship the proof and the plan in the same evening, and then the plan stopped being speculation and became a punch list.

That, more than any specific feature in any specific tool, is what I think people mean when they say working with these models is different from working without them. It isn't that you write code faster. It's that the gap between "an idea worth checking" and "an artifact you can hold and critique" collapses to something like an afternoon, and a lot of ideas worth checking turn out to be ideas worth shipping.

## What's Actually Running

The system as it stands today (it's still alpha; consider this a status update, not a marketing page) is roughly what the feasibility study said it would be:

- A `gateway` binary takes Forgejo webhooks, HMAC-verifies them, and enqueues jobs.
- An orchestrator runs each PR through a deterministic pipeline: shallow clone, LLM-driven triage that skips lockfile-only PRs and routes trivial files away from the reasoning tier, fan-out across 45 bundled linters (each one sandboxed via Podman, no network, read-only mount, dropped capabilities; the Kudelski lesson is encoded as a hard architectural rule), tree-sitter symbol extraction, embedding-based retrieval over a persistent learnings store, a strict-JSON-schema review prompt with a self-heal retry, and a cheap-tier verifier that drops findings the LLM can't ground in the cited code.
- An `@auto_review` chat handler takes natural-language commands on inline review threads — `re-review`, `remember <text>`, `forget <id>`, `autofix`, `docstring`, `tests`, plus free-form questions — with a polling fallback for the Gitea/Forgejo bug where `pull_request_review_comment` events don't fire reliably,[^gitea-26023] if you're curious why a webhook-driven service needs a poller bolted to its side.
- A `bench` CLI subcommand replays PR fixtures through the review path, so model upgrades and prompt changes can be measured against a regression corpus instead of vibes.
- The whole thing builds and tests through one `nix flake check`, and CI runs the same derivations bit-for-bit.

There are around a thousand tests. The pipeline is dogfooding itself. The threat model and ADRs are in `docs/`.

I'm writing this while `auto_review` is reviewing the PR for the article-publishing change behind it, which is the kind of recursive moment that would have made me roll my eyes in someone else's blog post a year ago. I'd still roll my eyes at it. That doesn't make it less true.

## What Generalizes

The techniques that made this work aren't specific to code review. They apply to any agentic system you're trying to deploy in production without burning a hole in your budget or your security posture. Most of them cut against how the early "give the model some tools and a loop and let it run" wave taught us to build these things. Each one earned its place by saving the project from a different failure mode.

**1. A multi-stage pipeline beats a pure ReAct loop, by a lot.** The naïve agentic shape is "give the LLM a set of tools, prompt it with a goal, let it loop until it claims it's done." It's also the shape that runs five minutes per request, costs ten times what it should, returns answers you can't reproduce, and falls over the moment one of its turns hallucinates a tool call. CodeRabbit's public engineering writing is firm on this, and my own experience confirms it: production agentic systems should be deterministic pipelines with agentic spans embedded inside them, not agents with deterministic helpers hanging off them. In `auto_review`, the orchestrator does the routing, the cloning, the linter fan-out, the context curation, the dedup, and the result-posting in straight Rust. The LLM is invoked only at the points where actual judgement is required: triage, summarization, review, verification. Each invocation has a defined input shape, a defined output shape, and a defined retry policy. The "agent" is contained inside one or two stages of the pipeline, not draped over the whole thing. This makes the system cheaper, faster, debuggable, and rerunnable. It also lets you mix model tiers (see lesson 2), which a pure ReAct loop structurally can't do, because the same loop is doing all the work.

**2. Two-tier model routing buys you 50% of your inference cost back.** Most of what you think of as "agent work" isn't actually hard. It's classification, summarization, routing, format conversion. None of those need a reasoning model. All of them are well within the capability of a small fast model that costs an order of magnitude less per token. The trick (CodeRabbit was the first place I saw it written down clearly) is to put a cheap-tier model in front of the reasoning tier, with the cheap one's job being "decide whether this case is even worth thinking about hard." In `auto_review`, the cheap-tier triage classifier looks at every PR and decides which files are trivial (lockfile updates, formatting-only changes, generated code) versus which warrant the reasoning tier. Trivial files are reviewed by the cheap model or skipped entirely; the rest get the expensive treatment. The same pattern recurs at the verification stage: a cheap-tier model passes over each finding the reasoning tier produced and asks, "does the cited code actually do what the finding claims?" The cheap model is wrong sometimes, but its errors are bounded and asymmetric. It drops false positives, which is what you want. The cumulative effect on cost is roughly a factor of two. The cumulative effect on quality is positive, because the reasoning tier is no longer being asked to spend tokens on cases beneath its dignity. This generalizes to almost any agentic problem: a cheap classifier that decides whether to escalate is better than a single expensive model that handles everything.

**3. Strict-JSON-schema output with a bounded self-heal loop is the only way to talk to a model in production.** "JSON mode" alone isn't enough. You want a real schema validator on the way out (pydantic, serde, whatever your language gives you), and you want the validation error itself to be fed back to the model on retry. In `auto_review`, every LLM call that produces structured output is wrapped in a self-heal pattern: validate, on failure feed the failure message back as the next user turn, retry up to N times, then fail loudly. This is the difference between a system that fails 5% of the time and a system that fails 0.1% of the time. The bounded retry is non-negotiable; you don't want unbounded retry budgets in a paid-per-token system. The loud failure when the budget is exhausted is the right behavior. Better to surface a real error than ship corrupted state. The general principle: the LLM is an external system at a trust boundary, and trust boundaries get parsers, validators, and retries, just like every other untrusted input you've ever handled.

**4. Verification belongs in a separate pass, run by a different (cheaper) model.** This was, for me, the single most counterintuitive technique in the CodeRabbit architecture, and it has reshaped how I think about LLM systems generally. Most people start with the intuition that verification is part of the generation step: the same model that produces the answer should also check it. That turns out to be wrong in two ways. First, the model that produced the answer is biased toward defending it. Second, generation is the expensive step and verification is the cheap one, so you want to do them with different tools. In `auto_review`, the reasoning tier produces a list of candidate findings against a strict schema. The cheap tier then takes each finding individually, in isolation, and is asked: "given the cited file, line range, and snippet, does the claim in the finding actually hold?" Findings that fail this check get dropped. The result is that hallucinated criticisms, which are the dominant failure mode of LLM code review, disappear before they're posted. The general lesson: in any agentic system, separate the generative model from the validating model, and make the validator as adversarial as you can afford. Skepticism is cheap; generation is expensive. Front-load the skepticism.

**5. RAG is one input among many, not a panacea.** "Just embed the codebase, pull top-K, and prompt the model" is the demo version of retrieval-augmented generation, and it's roughly a third of what a production system actually needs. CodeRabbit's writing makes this clear, and `auto_review`'s context-curation stage reflects it directly. The prompt is assembled from tree-sitter symbol queries (which are better than embedding retrieval for "where is this function defined and where is it used"), embedding retrieval over a persistent learnings store (which captures team-specific judgement no public model has ever seen), a co-change graph (which surfaces files that historically move together), and the diff itself. Pure vector retrieval over the codebase as a flat blob is shallow and noisy. The production pattern is multi-modal retrieval converging on a curated context the generative model can actually use. Don't assume a single retrieval modality is sufficient for a real-world agentic problem. Build the system to compose retrievals from multiple structurally different sources.

**6. Memory is a separate store from context, and it must be small.** A "learnings" store (per-context, structured, persistently writable, queryable by similarity) is what makes the agent get better over time without retraining anything. In `auto_review`, the chat handler accepts `remember <text>` and `forget <id>` so a maintainer can teach the bot about local conventions, and the learnings are surfaced into the review prompt for future PRs in that repo. The total token count of the learnings store added to a given prompt is capped low, single-digit kilobytes, because more is worse, not better. Agents need memory, but memory isn't context window. Structured, named, scoped, queryable, deletable memory is a different problem from "stuff more of the chat into the prompt," and the two shouldn't be conflated. The systems that get this right are the ones where memory is a first-class object the user can audit and edit.

**7. Sandbox the tools or you have a remote-code-execution vulnerability.** This is the Kudelski lesson, generalized. The moment your agent can issue tool calls that execute code (`bash`, `eval`, "run the linter on this PR's config file"), you're accepting attacker-controlled input through that surface, because any user who can submit a PR can put adversarial content into a file the tool will then process. The case study CodeRabbit and Kudelski Security have both publicly written up: at the time of the January 2025 disclosure, Rubocop was running outside CodeRabbit's sandbox, and Rubocop's documented `require: - ./ext.rb` extension directive in `.rubocop.yml` loaded arbitrary Ruby, which Kudelski's researchers used to extract CodeRabbit's GitHub-App private key and demonstrate write access to approximately a million repositories. In `auto_review`, every linter invocation and every LLM-issued shell call goes through `ar-sandbox`, which in production runs Podman with `--network=none --read-only --cap-drop=ALL --security-opt=no-new-privileges` and a capped wall-clock budget. There's a less-safe `DirectSandbox` for local development, and the gateway logs a hard warning at boot if it's selected. The general lesson, which applies to every agentic system that issues tool calls touching anything user-supplied: sandbox at the OS level, not at the prompt level. "We told the model not to do that" isn't a security control; the kernel is.

**8. Test against fakes, not the real model.** LLM tests against the live API are slow, flaky, expensive, and non-deterministic. LLM tests with HTTP-level mocks are brittle to vendor changes and miss most of the interesting failure modes. The pattern that works in `auto_review` is `CannedProvider` and `ScriptedProvider`: fakes implemented at the same trait the real providers implement, returning canned responses in the test's order, with assertions over the prompts the system would have sent. This is fast, deterministic, runs offline, and exercises the actual control flow. In an agentic system, the LLM is a dependency. Inject it. Fake it for tests. Don't test against the real one in CI.

**9. Make the inference layer provider-agnostic from day one.** The OpenAI chat-completion API is the de-facto standard, and the right abstraction is a small trait with a couple of methods (chat-completion, embedding) and an enum of model tiers (Reasoning, Cheap, Embedding) that maps to provider implementations. `auto_review`'s `ar-llm::Router` does exactly this, and it pays for itself the first time someone wants to mix a local Ollama embedding model with a cloud reasoning model, which, if you're deploying agentic systems on real teams' infrastructure, will be approximately every team. Don't build assumptions about the model vendor into your business logic. The vendor will change. Cheaper providers will appear. Local inference will get better. The cost of the abstraction is a few hundred lines; the cost of not having it is a fork every time you switch.

**10. A regression bench is the only honest way to upgrade prompts or models.** "It feels better" isn't a release criterion. `auto_review` ships a `bench` CLI subcommand that replays a fixture set of PRs through the full review path against a chosen model, scores precision and recall against a labeled ground truth, and prints a report. When I bump the cheap-tier model from one snapshot to another, I run the bench. When I edit the review prompt, I run the bench. Every agentic system needs a regression harness, because LLM behavior changes under upgrades and edits in non-obvious ways, and the only protection against silently regressing is to measure before and after. If your system doesn't have a bench, you're flying blind.

None of these techniques is novel by itself. What's new is that the combination is what makes a system work in production. Take any one of them out and the system gets noticeably worse. Pull the verifier and you get hallucinated criticisms. Pull the schema validator and you get parse-error 500s. Pull the sandbox and you get an RCE. Pull the bench and you get silent regressions. They interlock, and the interlock is the architecture. If you're building anything agentic and ambitious, this is the shape I'd start with, regardless of whether your problem is code review or something else entirely.

## Sovereignty as a First-Class Feature

This is the part the closed-source incumbents can't match, and it's the part I want to underline.

`auto_review` is a single binary you run next to your Forgejo instance. The LLM router is provider-agnostic. Anything that speaks the OpenAI chat-completion protocol works. That includes Ollama, vLLM, OpenRouter, Together, Groq, and yes, hosted OpenAI / Anthropic / whoever you like. It also includes "your own local server with a 32B-class model and no internet." There's an explicit profile in the feasibility study and a verification target in the threat model for the all-local case: a full review pipeline completing on a workstation with Ollama and no egress. No code, no diffs, no embeddings, no learnings ever leave your machine.

You can also mix and match. Local embeddings (cheap, fast on consumer GPUs) plus a cloud reasoning tier for the heavy lift. A BYOK setup where your reviewer hits your OpenAI account against your prompts inside your sandbox. Whatever your data-residency and budget posture demand.

This isn't an accident of the architecture; it is the architecture. I'm never going to be in a position to monetize your code, and neither is anyone else, because the system is a tool you run, not a service you rent.

## What I'm Releasing, and Why

One caveat before anything else: [`auto_review`](https://git.johnwilger.com/jwilger/auto_review) is **pre-0.1.0**. It's alpha software with rough edges and almost certainly some bugs I haven't tripped over yet. The threat model is written down but not yet fully exercised against an adversarial PR corpus. The deployment story works on my hardware and probably not yet on yours without some hand-holding. **Use it at your own risk.** I'm working this week and next to push it to a usable, secure, default-deployable first release: finishing the production-polish items in the threat model, running a precision-recall benchmark against a labelled corpus of historical PRs, hardening the operator path, settling the license. If you want to follow along, the repository is open at [git.johnwilger.com/jwilger/auto_review](https://git.johnwilger.com/jwilger/auto_review) and the issues are public. If you want to wait for the 0.1.0 tag, that's reasonable.

`auto_review` will be open source under a permissive license. Once it tags 0.1.0, if you're already on Forgejo (or Gitea, which uses mostly the same API), you'll be able to `nix run` or `docker compose up` your own instance next to your forge and have it working on real PRs the same afternoon. If you're not on Forgejo, this is one more reason to consider it.

I'm not pretending this is a CodeRabbit replacement yet. CodeRabbit has years of accumulated craft and an enormous corpus of real-world tuning that a single engineer can't match in an evening. What I'm claiming is that the architecture is reproducible from public writing, the core pieces work, the gap is closable, and I'm going to close it in the open, with help from anyone who wants to pitch in.

## Tying Off The Threads

I left GitHub because the platform stopped serving me, and the public record now reads like a textbook case of the pattern Cory Doctorow named. The April merge-queue regression that retroactively un-merged two thousand pull requests, the days-long Elasticsearch outage, the malicious npm brand-squat sitting on a registry whose owner couldn't be moved to enforce its own trademark rules until secrets walked out the front door, the slipping uptime, the public apology from the CTO: none of that is going to spontaneously reverse. The owner has thirty years of experience operating things at scale and has chosen not to use it here. I'm done waiting for that to change.

The architectural moat around closed-source developer tools, on the other hand, turns out to be shallower than it used to be. The knowledge required to build the next CodeRabbit is, by and large, public: case studies, customer stories, security disclosures, conference talks, podcasts. The vendors talk about their systems because their marketing requires them to. A capable model with web access can read all of it at once and triangulate the decisions that mattered, sharply enough to draft a real plan a single engineer can critique on its own terms. The remaining moat is execution, taste, and care, and execution is where small focused efforts can compete on quality precisely because they aren't being optimized to extract margin from a customer base.

`auto_review` is going to live next to your forge, not next to my AWS bill. The architecture is the sovereignty. An OpenAI-compatible inference abstraction means your prompts, your diffs, your embeddings, and your accumulated learnings don't have to leave your machine. The gates between you and that future are no longer technical; they're configuration. I'm never going to be in a position to monetize your code, because the system is a tool you run, not a service you rent.

And the techniques that made any of this work — pipelines over loops, two-tier model routing, schema validation with bounded retry, separate cheap-tier verification, multi-modal retrieval, sandboxed tools, fakes for tests, regression benches — generalize. They're how I'd build the next agentic system, whatever it does. I think a lot of next-generation agentic systems are going to converge on roughly this shape, whether we coordinate on it or not, because the alternatives turn out to be fragile in production. If this essay shortens that convergence by one project for one team, that's worth the writing.

The feasibility study said half a year. The first end-to-end thread took an evening. I don't want to overgeneralize from one project, but I don't want to underclaim either. If you've been waiting to find out whether the tool you wish existed is buildable by you, the answer is, more often than it used to be, yes.
